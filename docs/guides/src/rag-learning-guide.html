1. What you built

A command-line app that answers questions about a folder of documents. For every question it finds the most relevant passages, gives them to an LLM, and returns an answer where every fact is tagged with a citation like [1] pointing to the file and section it came from. If the documents don't contain the answer, it says "I couldn't find that in the documents."

python ask.py "I left my laptop in a cab, and how long must my password be?"

- Report the lost laptop to the security team within 1 hour by calling the
  IT helpdesk on extension 4040 so they can lock and wipe it remotely. [2]
- Your Orbit password must be at least 14 characters long. [1]

Sources:
  [1] it-security.md > Passwords and sign-in
  [2] it-security.md > Lost or stolen devices

The demo data is a fictional company handbook (Orbit Coffee Co.) with four documents. You can drop your own .md, .txt or .pdf files into rag/data/.

What this project proves to an employer. You understand the full retrieval pipeline (not just "call LangChain"), you know why hybrid search and reranking exist, and you measure quality with an evaluation set instead of guessing. You also hit a real failure, diagnosed it with numbers, and fixed it.

2. Why RAG exists

An LLM only knows what was in its training data. That causes four problems for real applications:

ProblemExampleHow RAG helps
Private dataIt has never seen your company's leave policy.We hand it the relevant policy text at question time.
Out of dateTraining stopped months ago; the policy changed last week.Re-index the files; no retraining needed.
HallucinationAsked about something it doesn't know, it invents a plausible answer."Answer only from these sources" + citations the user can check.
Context costYou can't paste 10,000 pages into every prompt.Send only the 4 most relevant chunks (~800 tokens).

Retrieval-Augmented Generation = Retrieve relevant text → Augment the prompt with it → let the model Generate the answer. The model becomes a reader of your documents rather than a memory of the internet.

RAG vs fine-tuning (common interview question). Fine-tuning changes the model's weights. It's good for teaching a style or format, but bad for facts: it's expensive to update, can't cite sources, and still hallucinates. RAG is the default choice for "answer questions about our documents".

3. The big picture

A RAG system has two phases. Indexing runs once (and again whenever documents change). Answering runs for every question.

Key insight. If retrieval doesn't find the right chunk, no LLM can answer correctly. That's why most of this project (and most real RAG work) is about retrieval quality, and why we evaluate retrieval separately.

4. Chunking

We don't search whole documents; we search chunks, passages of up to CHUNK_MAX_CHARS = 800 characters (~150–200 words). Why not whole documents? A 50-page PDF would match almost any question weakly, and pasting all of it into the prompt would be slow and expensive. Why not single sentences? "You get 10 days." doesn't say 10 days of what.

How our chunker works (documents.py)

  1. Split into paragraphs at blank lines. A paragraph is a natural unit of meaning, so we avoid cutting inside one.
  2. Track headings. Every paragraph is tagged with the Markdown heading above it (e.g. Sick leave).
  3. Pack greedily. Keep adding paragraphs to the current chunk until the next one won't fit, then start a new chunk.
  4. Never mix sections. A new heading always starts a new chunk, so one chunk = one topic.
  5. Overlap. A new chunk (within the same section) starts with the last ~150 characters of the previous one, so a fact sitting on the boundary is complete in at least one chunk.
  6. Giant paragraphs are split at sentence ends, or at spaces if one "sentence" is still too long (hard_split).

Contextual chunk headers

Before embedding, each chunk is prefixed with where it came from:

# Chunk.embedding_text()
leave-policy.md > Sick leave
You get 10 days of paid sick leave per year. A doctor's note is only required if ...

The word "sick" now appears in the searchable text even if the paragraph itself never said it. This cheap trick noticeably improves both vector and keyword search.

Tuning trade-off. Smaller chunks = more precise matches but less context per chunk. Bigger chunks = more context but diluted matches and more prompt tokens. 500–1,000 characters with 10–20% overlap is a common starting point. Change it in config.py, re-run ingest.py and evaluate.py, and let the numbers decide.

5. Embeddings & vector search

An embedding is a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a text. Our model, BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5, turns any text into 384 numbers. It was trained so that texts with similar meaning get similar numbers: "Can I bring my puppy?" ends up close to "Dogs are welcome on Fridays", even though they share no words.

Cosine similarity

We compare two embeddings by the angle between them: cosine similarity = (a · b) / (|a| × |b|). It's 1 for the same direction (same meaning), around 0 for unrelated. fastembed returns vectors already normalised to length 1, so |a| = |b| = 1 and cosine similarity is just the dot product. That's why searching every chunk is one line of NumPy:

# retriever.py: vectors is a (27 chunks x 384) matrix, q is (384,)
sims = self.vectors @ q            # 27 similarity scores in one matrix multiply
top = np.argsort(-sims)[:k]        # indices of the k highest scores

Query vs passage embeddings

BGE models expect a question to be embedded slightly differently from a document (a short instruction is prepended). That's why the code uses query_embed() for questions and passage_embed() for chunks. Questions and answers are phrased differently ("How many days…?" vs "Employees receive 24 days…"), and this "asymmetric" setup accounts for that.

Do I need a vector database? Not for 27 chunks, or even 50,000: a brute-force NumPy dot product is milliseconds. Vector databases (pgvector, Qdrant, Chroma, Pinecone) earn their place with millions of vectors (they use approximate nearest neighbour indexes like HNSW), metadata filters, concurrent updates and persistence. Knowing when you don't need one is a good interview answer.
Weakness of embeddings: exact tokens. Codes (EXP-44), product names, IDs and rare acronyms often embed poorly, because the model has no idea what "EXP-44" means. That's the gap BM25 fills.

6. BM25 keyword search

BM25 is the ranking formula behind classic search engines (Elasticsearch, Lucene, OpenSearch). We wrote it from scratch in bm25.py (about 40 lines) so nothing is magic. For a query, each chunk's score is the sum over query words t:

score(chunk) = Σ  IDF(t) × tf × (k1 + 1) / ( tf + k1 × (1 − b + b × len / avg_len) )

tf      = how many times word t appears in this chunk
IDF(t)  = log(1 + (N − df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))   N = chunks, df = chunks containing t
len     = words in this chunk, avg_len = average over all chunks
k1=1.5, b=0.75 (standard defaults)
PartIntuition
IDF (inverse document frequency)Rare words matter more. "EXP-44" appears in 1 chunk → big weight. "leave" appears in many → small weight.
tf with k1 (term frequency, saturating)Mentioning a word 2× is better than 1×, but 20× isn't 20× better. k1 controls how fast it saturates.
Length normalisation (b)A short chunk that mentions "VPN" is probably about VPNs; a long one may mention it in passing.

Tokenisation matters: we lowercase, keep hyphenated codes like exp-44 as one token, and drop stopwords ("the", "how", "is"…) that carry no meaning. (Section 13 shows what happens when a stopword slips through.)

BM25 is good at: exact codes, names, numbers, rare jargon. It's fast and needs no model.
BM25 is bad at: synonyms and paraphrases. "refund" ≠ "reimbursed", "vacation" ≠ "annual leave", "puppy" ≠ "dog".

7. Hybrid search & Reciprocal Rank Fusion

Vector search understands meaning; BM25 catches exact words. Hybrid search runs both and merges the results. The catch: their scores are on different scales (cosine ≈ 0.4–0.8, BM25 ≈ 0–20), so you can't just add them.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) ignores the raw scores and uses only each chunk's rank in each list:

RRF(chunk) = Σ over lists   1 / (60 + rank in that list)        # 60 = RRF_K, the standard constant

A chunk ranked #1 in one list gets 1/61 = 0.0164. Ranked #1 in both: 0.0328. The constant 60 flattens the curve so a single #1 can't dominate. Agreement between the two methods is what wins.

Worked example: where hybrid went wrong

Real numbers from our index for the question "When will my expense refund arrive?" (the answer, "paid on the 28th", lives in the Submitting a claim chunk):

ChunkVector rank (cosine)BM25 rank (score)RRF scoreHybrid rank
Expense codes#2 (0.625)#1 (3.28)1/62 + 1/61 = 0.03251
Booking travel#4 (0.585)#4 (1.88)0.03122
Lost or stolen devices#7 (0.534)#2 (2.19)0.03113
Sick leave#15 (0.475)#3 (1.93)0.02924
Submitting a claim ✔#1 (0.707)not found (0)1/61 = 0.01645 → cut off

Vector search got it right, but the document says "reimbursed", never "refund", so BM25 didn't find the right chunk at all. BM25 instead ranked chunks that happened to share words like "expense" and "will". Those chunks appeared in both lists, so their small scores added up and pushed the correct answer out of the top 4.

Lesson. Hybrid is not automatically better. RRF rewards agreement, so when one method is confidently right and the other is confidently noisy, fusion can hurt. You only discover this by evaluating. The fix is a stage that actually reads the candidates: a reranker.

8. Reranking (two-stage retrieval)

Our embedding model is a bi-encoder: it encodes the question and each chunk separately, then compares vectors. That's fast (chunk vectors are computed once, in advance), but the model never sees the question and the chunk together.

A cross-encoder takes question + chunk as one input and outputs a relevance score. It can notice that "refund arrive" is answered by "reimbursed with your next salary payment on the 28th". It's much more accurate, but must run once per (question, chunk) pair at question time, which is too slow for a million chunks.

Bi-encoder (embeddings)Cross-encoder (reranker)
InputQuestion and chunk separatelyQuestion + chunk together
Precompute chunks?Yes, at indexing timeNo, runs per question
SpeedVery fast, scales to millionsSlow; use on ~20–100 candidates
AccuracyGoodBetter
Our modelbge-small-en-v1.5ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 (80 MB)

So we combine them: stage 1 (hybrid) quickly fetches 20 candidates with high recall; stage 2 (reranker) carefully orders them for precision, and we keep the top 4.

# retriever.py: rerank_search
candidates = self.hybrid_search(query, config.RERANK_CANDIDATES)          # 20, fast
scores = list(reranker().rerank(query, [c.embedding_text() for c, _ in candidates]))
ranked = sorted(zip(candidates, scores), key=lambda pair: -pair[1])[:k]   # best 4

On the refund question the reranker put Submitting a claim first by a wide margin (score −2.6 vs −10.0 for the next). Reranker scores are raw "logits": only their order matters, and negative values are normal.

9. Prompting for cited answers

The retrieved chunks are numbered and placed in the user message; the rules go in the system prompt (ask.py):

System: You answer questions using ONLY the numbered sources provided.
- After every fact, cite the source number in square brackets, like [1] or [2][3].
- If the sources do not contain the answer, say: "I couldn't find that in the
  documents." Do not guess or use outside knowledge.
- Be concise.

User: Sources:
[1] (from expenses-and-travel.md, section "Submitting a claim")
Submit claims in Brewbook ... reimbursed with your next salary payment on the 28th ...
[2] (from ...)
Question: When will my expense refund arrive?
  • Numbered sources let the model cite and let us print a source list the user can verify. Trust comes from checkability.
  • An explicit "I don't know" sentence gives the model a safe exit instead of inventing an answer. We tested it: "What is the CEO's salary?" → "I couldn't find that in the documents."
  • temperature = 0.1: we want faithful extraction, not creativity.
  • Streaming prints tokens as they arrive, the same technique as Nova.

10. Evaluation

Without measurement, every change is a guess. eval/questions.json holds 18 test questions, each with the file that holds the answer and a phrase that must appear in the correct chunk:

{"question": "Am I allowed to bring my puppy to work?",
 "source": "office-and-culture.md", "expected": "Fridays"}

The questions are deliberately mixed: some share words with the documents ("What is expense code EXP-44 used for?"), others are paraphrases ("vacation days" for "annual leave", "pen drive" for "USB storage", "refund" for "reimbursed").

The two metrics

MetricMeaningExample (3 questions, correct chunk at rank 1, 2, missing)
Hit@kShare of questions where a correct chunk is anywhere in the top k. "Could the LLM possibly answer?"2 of 3 found → 67%
MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank)Average of 1/rank (0 if missing). Rewards putting the answer first.(1 + ½ + 0) / 3 = 0.50

Our results (top-4)

MethodHit@4MRRObservation
BM25 only94%0.78Fails on paraphrase ("refund"); often ranks the answer 2nd
Vector only100%0.96Strong, but "which days in the office" was only #4
Hybrid (RRF)94%0.94Fixed the office question, broke the refund question
Hybrid + rerank100%1.00Correct chunk ranked first on every question
Be honest about this. 18 questions that I wrote myself over 4 short documents is a regression test, not a benchmark. 100% here does not mean 100% on real data. Say this in interviews; it shows maturity. To make it stronger: add 50+ questions, include questions with no answer, and use questions written by someone else.

Note that we evaluate retrieval only. Evaluating the answers (faithfulness: is every claim supported by the cited source?) usually uses an "LLM-as-judge"; that's a planned next project.

documents.py: the packing loop

for section, para in split_into_blocks(text):
    for piece in hard_split(para, max_chars) if len(para) > max_chars else [para]:
        if section != current_section:              # new heading: always a new chunk
            flush(); current, current_section = piece, section
        elif len(current) + len(piece) + 2 > max_chars:   # won't fit: new chunk
            flush()
            tail = current[-overlap_chars:]         # overlap from previous chunk
            tail = tail[tail.find(" ") + 1:]         # start at a word boundary
            fits = tail and len(tail) + len(piece) + 2 <= max_chars
            current = f"{tail}\n\n{piece}" if fits else piece
        else:
            current = f"{current}\n\n{piece}"        # fits: keep packing

flush() is a nested function that appends the current text as a Chunk. nonlocal lets it modify the outer variable.

retriever.py: hybrid search

pool = max(k * 5, 20)                     # look deeper than k before merging
fused = {}
for results in (self.vector_search(query, pool), self.bm25_search(query, pool)):
    for rank, (chunk, _) in enumerate(results, start=1):
        fused[chunk.id] = fused.get(chunk.id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (config.RRF_K + rank)
top = sorted(fused, key=lambda i: -fused[i])[:k]

What the index looks like on disk

index/vectors.npy   NumPy array, shape (27, 384), float32: one row per chunk
index/chunks.json   [{"id": 0, "source": "expenses-and-travel.md",
                      "section": "Booking travel", "text": "All business trips ..."}, ...]
index/meta.json     which embedding model and chunk settings built it

Row i of the matrix is the embedding of chunk i; that shared position is the only link between the two files. meta.json exists because mixing vectors from two different models is a silent bug: the numbers would be meaningless, but nothing would crash.

  2. Remove the header from embedding_text() (return only self.text). Which questions get worse?
  3. Add "will" to STOPWORDS and look at BM25's ranking for the refund question again.
  4. Set RRF_K = 1. Now rank #1 is worth far more than #2. Does hybrid improve?

13. Problems we hit (real engineering lessons)

1. The Gemini API key was rejected. The plan was to use Gemini's embedding API. The key in chatbot/.env returned API_KEY_INVALID. Instead of blocking, we switched to local embeddings with fastembed (ONNX models on the CPU). This turned out better for a portfolio: free, offline, no rate limits, and anyone can run the project with only a Groq key. Lesson: keep provider choices swappable behind one function (embed_passages).
2. Hybrid search made one question worse. Section 7 in full. Found only because evaluate.py compared methods side by side. Lesson: "best practice" components must still be measured on your data.
3. The stopword leak. BM25 ranked Lost or stolen devices #2 for the refund question. Why? Both contain the word "will" ("You will not be blamed…"), and "will" isn't in our stopword list. Lesson: inspect why wrong results rank high; the cause is often mundane.
4. Windows console crash: UnicodeEncodeError: 'charmap' codec can't encode ' '. The model output a narrow no-break space, and the Windows console defaults to the old cp1252 encoding. Fix: sys.stdout.reconfigure(encoding="utf-8", errors="replace"). Lesson: LLM output is untrusted text: any Unicode character can appear.
5. Oversized chunks. A test with very long paragraphs showed chunks of 379 characters when the limit was 300: overlap was added on top of a full-size piece. Fix: only prepend the overlap if the result still fits; and hard_split now falls back to splitting at spaces when a "sentence" has no full stop. Lesson: test edge cases of your own utilities with synthetic input.

15. Interview questions (with answers)

Q1. What is RAG and when would you use it instead of fine-tuning?
Retrieve relevant documents and put them in the prompt so the model answers from them. Use it for knowledge that is private, changes often, or must be cited. Fine-tuning is for behaviour/style/format, not facts.
Q2. How do you choose chunk size?
Trade-off between precision (small) and context (large). Start around 500–1,000 characters with 10–20% overlap, respect natural boundaries (paragraphs, headings), then tune with a retrieval eval set.
Q3. What is cosine similarity, and why is a dot product enough here?
The cosine of the angle between two vectors: 1 means same direction (meaning). When vectors are normalised to length 1, the dot product equals the cosine.
Q4. Why combine keyword and vector search?
Vectors handle synonyms and paraphrase; BM25 handles exact tokens like codes and names. Each covers the other's blind spot.
Q5. How does Reciprocal Rank Fusion work, and why not just add the scores?
Score = Σ 1/(k + rank) across lists, k≈60. Raw scores are on incompatible scales; ranks are comparable. Caveat: it rewards agreement, so a result found by only one retriever can be outvoted (our refund example).
Q6. Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder?
A bi-encoder embeds query and document separately (fast, precomputable, used for retrieval). A cross-encoder reads them together (slow, more accurate, used to rerank a short candidate list).
Q7. How do you evaluate a RAG system?
Separately: retrieval (Hit@k / recall@k, MRR, nDCG on labelled questions) and generation (faithfulness to sources, answer correctness, correct refusal when the answer is absent), often with an LLM judge plus spot checks.
Q8. How do you reduce hallucinations in RAG?
Better retrieval (the main cause is missing context), instructions to answer only from sources, an explicit "I don't know" option, required citations, low temperature, and faithfulness checks.
Q9. When do you need a vector database?
When brute force gets too slow (roughly millions of vectors), or you need ANN indexes, metadata filtering, concurrent writes, persistence and access control. For small corpora, a NumPy matrix is fine.
Q10. Tell me about a bug or surprise in this project.
Adding hybrid search made one question fail: the docs said "reimbursed", the user said "refund". BM25 returned noise, and RRF let that noise outvote vector search's correct #1. I found it via per-method evaluation and fixed it with a cross-encoder reranker: Hit@4 94% → 100%, MRR 0.94 → 1.00.
