# Reading financial statements

Every listed company publishes three core financial statements. Together they show whether the business is growing, profitable, financially strong, and actually generating cash.

## Income statement (profit and loss)

The income statement covers a period (a quarter or a year) and flows from top to bottom:

- Revenue (sales): money earned from selling products or services.
- Gross profit: revenue minus the direct cost of making what was sold.
- Operating income (EBIT): gross profit minus operating expenses such as salaries, rent and marketing.
- Net income (profit after tax): what is left after interest and taxes. This is the "earnings" in earnings per share.
- EPS (earnings per share): net income divided by the number of shares. Diluted EPS also counts shares that could be created from options and convertibles.

## Margins

Margins show how much of each unit of revenue becomes profit.

- Gross margin = gross profit / revenue. High gross margins (above 50%) are typical of software and strong brands.
- Operating margin = operating income / revenue. It shows how efficiently the core business runs. Indian IT services companies have historically had operating margins of roughly 20% to 26%.
- Net profit margin = net income / revenue.

Rising margins usually signal pricing power or better efficiency; falling margins can signal competition or rising costs.

## Balance sheet

The balance sheet is a snapshot on one date. Assets = liabilities + shareholders' equity.

- Assets: cash, receivables, inventory, property, investments.
- Liabilities: debt, payables and other obligations.
- Shareholders' equity (book value): what would belong to owners if all assets were sold and all liabilities paid.

## Cash flow statement

Profit is an accounting figure; cash flow shows real money moving.

- Operating cash flow: cash generated by the core business.
- Capital expenditure (capex): cash spent on long-term assets like factories and equipment.
- Free cash flow (FCF) = operating cash flow - capex. This is cash available for dividends, buybacks, debt repayment or growth.

A company whose net income keeps rising while operating cash flow stays flat or falls deserves extra scrutiny, because profits are not turning into cash.

## Key health ratios

- Return on equity (ROE) = net income / shareholders' equity. It shows how well the company uses owners' money. Above 15% is generally considered good; above 25% is excellent. Very high ROE can also come from heavy debt or buybacks shrinking equity.
- Debt-to-equity = total debt / shareholders' equity. Below 0.5 is conservative; above 2 is high for most non-financial companies. Banks and NBFCs naturally have high leverage and are judged with different metrics.
- Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities. Above 1 means short-term assets cover short-term obligations.
- Interest coverage = operating income / interest expense. Below 2 can signal difficulty servicing debt.

## Growth

Year-on-year (YoY) growth compares a period with the same period a year earlier, which removes seasonal effects. Consistent revenue and earnings growth over several years matters more than one strong quarter.

## Red flags in financial statements

- Revenue growing but cash flow from operations falling.
- Receivables growing much faster than revenue (customers may not be paying).
- Debt rising quickly without matching growth in earnings.
- Frequent "one-time" or "exceptional" items that are not really one-time.
- Large related-party transactions or repeated changes of auditor.
- Promoter (founder) shareholding being pledged as collateral, which is disclosed by Indian companies.
