# Valuation ratios

Valuation ratios compare a company's share price with what the business earns or owns. They answer the question "how much am I paying for each rupee or dollar of profit, sales or assets?" A ratio is only meaningful when compared with the same company's history, its direct competitors, or its sector average.

## Price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio

P/E = share price / earnings per share (EPS). A P/E of 20 means investors pay 20 for every 1 of yearly profit per share.

The trailing P/E uses the last 12 months of actual earnings. The forward P/E uses analysts' estimates for the next 12 months. When the forward P/E is lower than the trailing P/E, analysts expect earnings to grow.

A high P/E usually means the market expects strong future growth, or that the stock is expensive. A low P/E can mean the stock is cheap, or that the market expects profits to fall (a "value trap"). P/E is meaningless when earnings are negative or unusually low because of one-off items.

Typical P/E levels differ by sector. Fast-growing technology and consumer brands often trade at 30 or more, while banks, utilities, metals and other cyclical businesses often trade between 8 and 15.

## PEG ratio

PEG = P/E divided by the expected annual earnings growth rate in percent. A company with a P/E of 30 and 30% expected growth has a PEG of 1.0. A PEG near 1 is often read as "fairly priced for its growth"; well above 2 suggests the price already assumes a lot of growth. PEG depends heavily on growth estimates, which are often wrong.

## Price-to-book (P/B) ratio

P/B = share price / book value per share, where book value is shareholders' equity (assets minus liabilities). P/B is most useful for asset-heavy businesses such as banks, insurers and NBFCs, where book value reflects real lending capital. For asset-light companies (software, IT services, consumer brands) a high P/B is normal, because their real value is in people, brands and software that the balance sheet does not record.

## EV/EBITDA

Enterprise value (EV) = market capitalisation + total debt - cash. It is the price of the whole business, including what is owed to lenders. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of operating profit.

EV/EBITDA lets you compare companies with different amounts of debt, which P/E cannot do fairly. Values of roughly 6 to 12 are common for mature businesses; growth companies trade higher.

## Dividend yield

Dividend yield = annual dividend per share / share price, shown as a percent. A yield of 2% means 2 in dividends per year for every 100 invested. A very high yield can be a warning sign: the price may have fallen sharply because the market expects the dividend to be cut.

## Market capitalisation

Market cap = share price x number of shares outstanding. In India, SEBI's classification ranks listed companies by market cap: the top 100 are large caps, 101 to 250 are mid caps, and the rest are small caps. Larger companies are usually more stable and more widely researched; smaller ones can grow faster but are riskier and less liquid.

## Common mistakes with valuation

- Comparing P/E across unrelated sectors.
- Treating a low P/E as automatically cheap without asking why earnings might fall.
- Ignoring debt: two companies with the same P/E can have very different risk if one carries heavy debt.
- Using one year of earnings that was inflated or depressed by one-off gains or losses.
- Forgetting that analyst targets and forward estimates are opinions and are frequently revised.
